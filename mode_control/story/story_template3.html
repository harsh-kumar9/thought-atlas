Steering how a model thinks

Reasoning models think differently on different kinds of tasks. We asked whether that difference sits in a part of the model we can turn, what turning it does, and whether reinforcement learning finds the same thing on its own.

Model Qwen3.5-9B · behaviour judge Gemma-4-31B · tasks: MATH-500, GPQA science questions, ACP planning, open-ended idea generation

Found 1

Each task has its own thinking signature. Math reasoning checks and sets subgoals; idea generation shifts perspective and backtracks.

Found 2

The signature can be steered. Adding one vector to the model's internal state while it thinks moves idea traces more than half way to the math profile.

Found 3

What steering buys is efficiency. On math, science and planning it reaches what unsteered thinking gets with 32k tokens using a third to a half of the tokens, and the model almost never loops.

Found 4

RL finds the same behaviour. Training only a steering vector with RL lands on the same way of thinking, through an unrelated direction. It keeps checking its work and is the most accurate arm at 32k.

Did not find

More accurate reasoning (on the problems it answers, A2 is right a little less often: 93% vs 97%), better ideas, or new kinds of solutions from steering.

Reasoning trace
The text the model writes in its thinking block before it answers.
Signature
A judge model labels every sentence with 8 behaviours (checking, setting a subgoal, asking itself a question…). The signature is how often each appears, per 100 sentences.
Steering vector
A fixed list of 4,096 numbers added to the model's internal state at one layer, at every token, while it thinks.
Token budget
The most tokens the model may write. If it has not answered by then, the task counts as wrong.
Random direction
A vector of the same size pointing somewhere random. It shows whether an effect comes from the direction or from any disturbance.
Grading
A judge model reads the final answer and we compare it with the reference. We checked it by hand on 100 traces.
Held-out problems
Problems never used to build or tune a vector. All accuracy numbers here are on held-out problems.
1

Models think differently on different tasks

What we did

We had Qwen3.5-9B solve four kinds of tasks, 200 tasks each, 4 samples per task. A judge model labelled every sentence of every reasoning trace.

Found

Math and planning lean on checking. Idea generation leans on perspective shifts, conflict and backtracking. Science questions sit in between. The difference is large: a simple classifier tells a math trace from an idea trace almost perfectly (AUROC 0.998).

2

There is a knob inside the model

What we did

We gave the model the same text twice: once with an instruction to explore, once with an instruction to check its work. The difference in its internal state is one direction. That direction also separates unprompted math traces from idea traces. Then we pushed along it while the model thought.

Found

Pushing for the first 64 tokens did nothing. Pushing at every token did. Idea traces moved more than half way toward the math signature, with fewer perspective shifts and backtracks and more checking. A random direction of the same size did not move them. The other way worked less well: math traces moved only a little toward the idea profile before the model started to loop.

3

What the knob buys depends on the task

What we did

We scored the final ideas for originality and feasibility, and the math answers for correctness, under each push. We also tried schedules, such as exploring first and checking later.

Found

On idea generation the knob is a dial between two kinds of ideas: toward exploring gives more original but less feasible ideas, toward checking gives the reverse. No push and no schedule improved both. On math, pushing toward exploring lowered accuracy.

4

A finer knob: steer toward what good math traces look like

What we did

The explore–check direction is one line through a space of eight behaviours. We fit a map from the model's internal state (layer 16) to the behaviours it is about to show. Then we took typical good math traces as targets and steered toward them. One target, called A2, asks for more subgoal setting with less checking and less self-questioning, a mix the explore–check knob cannot produce.

Found

Steering toward A2 raised math accuracy at a 12k-token budget from 57% to 81% on 199 held-out problems. The explore–check knob pushed toward checking reached 63%. A random direction of the same size lowered accuracy to 46%.

5

Is A2 better thinking, or faster thinking?

What we did

A 12k budget is tight: without steering, 42% of math traces had not answered yet. So we let every run think up to 32k tokens and read off accuracy at smaller budgets by cutting each trace. We also compared A2 with SEAL, a published method that steers reasoning to be shorter, and with a plain "Be concise." instruction.

Found

Mostly faster. With a 16k cap, A2 matches what unsteered thinking reaches with 32k (87% vs 85%), using 6.0k tokens on average instead of 13.2k. Its lead shrinks as the budget grows, from 27 points at 12k to 7 points at 32k. The lead that is left comes from finishing: at 32k, 12% of unsteered traces never answer (9% are stuck in a loop), against 1% for A2. On the problems each one answers, A2 is right a little less often (93% vs 97%), which fits it checking its work less. A2 beats SEAL by 22 points at 12k, and beats the concise instruction.

6

It carries over to other tasks with one right answer

What we did

We added the A2 vector, built from math, to the model on science questions (GPQA) and planning problems. We also built each task's own vector from its own good traces. Before running, we predicted the effects from which behaviours separate right from wrong answers on the same problem, and wrote the predictions down.

Found

The same pattern: large gains when the budget is tight, none when it is generous. The task's own vector does best. On GPQA it reaches the unsteered 32k accuracy (76% vs 74%) with 5.2k tokens instead of 16.0k; on planning, 92% vs 89% with 4.0k tokens instead of 10.2k. The predictions got the order right.

7

What changes in the thinking

What we did

We labelled the steered traces with the same judge and compared each with the unsteered trace for the same problem.

Found

On all three tasks the model asks itself fewer questions and sets more subgoals. On math it also checks less. The science and planning vectors cut self-questioning harder and leave checking alone, which fits what separates right from wrong answers in those tasks.

8

Many knobs, one behaviour

What we did

We compared the steering vectors two ways: how much they point the same way inside the model, and how much they change the thinking the same way.

Found

A2, SEAL's direction and a vector learned by RL (next section) point in unrelated directions inside the model: their overlap is close to zero. Yet they change the thinking in almost the same way. A random vector changes it the opposite way. The efficient way of thinking is a target that many different pushes reach.

9

Reinforcement learning finds the same behaviour

What we did

We froze the whole model and trained only a steering vector with reinforcement learning. The reward was 1 for a correct answer within 8k tokens. Each step used 16 problems with 8 samples each, drawn from the 301 MATH-500 problems outside the 199 we test on. We started once from zero and once from A2.

Found

From zero, RL learns a vector that matches A2 at 12k (79% vs 81%) and beats it at 32k (95% vs 92%), with no loops. It is a third as long and points somewhere unrelated, but it changes the thinking the same way, more gently, and with one difference: it does not cut checking. It finishes as often as A2 and is right as often as unsteered when it answers (97%). Started from A2, RL keeps A2 and sharpens it: 15 points better than A2 at 4k, and 2k fewer tokens at 32k.

10

Can steering produce different kinds of solutions?

What we did

On math, a different kind of solution can be worth more than the same solution found faster. Our steering never did this. Unprompted, the model uses the same method for a problem 95% of the time, and behaviour steering left the mix of methods unchanged. So we tried method prompts from Pólya's How to Solve It ("work backwards", "try small cases", "draw a picture"), and then steered along the prompt's own direction without the prompt.

Found

Prompts can change the method: "draw a picture" moves 29% of samples off the usual method. But a different method did not solve more problems. Mixing prompted and unprompted samples solves 86% of problems at least once in 4 tries, against 84% for unprompted samples alone, within noise. The prompt that did help, "work backwards" (67% to 76%), mostly kept the usual method and finished more often. Steering along the picture prompt's direction does worse: accuracy falls, loops rise, and the method barely changes.

Thinking has a signature that depends on the task, and part of it can be steered from inside the model. On tasks with one right answer, the part that pays off is efficiency: fewer self-questions, more subgoals, no loops. That gives the same accuracy for a third to a half of the tokens, more than a published steering method or a concise instruction gives. Reinforcement learning with a budgeted reward finds the same way of thinking on its own, through a different direction, and the vector it finds is the most accurate we have at 32k.

Steering did not make the reasoning itself more accurate. A2 answers far more often but is right a little less often on what it answers (93% vs 97%); its gain at large budgets comes from finishing. It did not make better ideas (it trades originality for feasibility), and it did not produce new kinds of solutions. A prompt can change the method, but here that did not solve more problems, and steering along the prompt's direction does not change the method.

Limits
  • One model (Qwen3.5-9B) and one behaviour judge (Gemma-4-31B). A second model is the first thing to add.
  • Most runs use one or two samples per task. Differences of a few points are within noise; the main effects are 15 to 38 points.
  • An earlier grader missed about a quarter of correct math answers. Every math number on this page has been re-graded (checked by hand on 100 traces, all right), except the rank-one knob's math result in section 3, which is still being re-graded.
  • The idea-quality judge scores most ideas near the top of its scale, which limits what it can detect.
